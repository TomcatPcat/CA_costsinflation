# Literature note: Wealth distribution, inflation taxes, and Canadian affordability

*Working literature memo for* Quantifying the Inflation Tax on Canadian Households.  
*Purpose:* situate the SFS adaptation of Wolff’s net inflation gain (\(NIG\)) accounting in three literatures—(a) measurement of wealth distributions and household incidence of asset-price / balance-sheet changes; (b) inflation and interest-rate redistribution, especially Wolff’s \(NIG\) / inflation-tax metrics; (c) Canadian wealth measurement and the policy focus on affordability (rates vs.\ inflation vs.\ other factors).

---

## (a) Measuring wealth distributions and how changes affect households

A large empirical literature documents that **portfolio composition differs systematically along the wealth distribution**, so asset-price and interest-rate movements redistribute wealth even when incomes move little.

**United States — SCF and historical surveys.**  
Kuhn, Schularick, and Steins (2020) construct the Historical Survey of Consumer Finances and show that postwar U.S.\ wealth dynamics are driven by housing-heavy middle-class portfolios versus equity-heavy rich portfolios; differential house and equity price paths decouple income and wealth inequality for long stretches ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/708815); [JPE](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/708815)).

**Federal Reserve SCF / Distributional Financial Accounts.**  
Bricker, Henriques, Krimmel, and Sabelhaus (2016) reconcile SCF and administrative tax-based top shares, clarifying how unit of analysis and wealth concepts affect measured concentration ([AER P&P](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.p20161020); longer FEDS/BPEA versions: [FEDS 2015-030](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/measuring-income-and-wealth-at-the-top-using-administrative-and-survey-data.htm)). Batty, Bricker, Briggs, et al.\ introduce the Distributional Financial Accounts (DFAs), which allocate Financial Accounts aggregates using SCF shares to produce timely quarterly wealth distributions ([FEDS 2019-017](https://doi.org/10.17016/FEDS.2019.017); NBER volume chapter Batty et al.\ 2022: [NBER](https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/measuring-distribution-and-mobility-income-and-wealth/distributional-financial-accounts-united-states)).

**Asset prices, rates, and welfare (theory + micro).**  
Gomez (2025) models the joint dynamics of asset prices and top wealth shares when the wealthy hold levered equity claims ([*Review of Economic Studies*](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdaf008); [PDF](https://www.matthieugomez.com/files/wealthinequality.pdf)). Gomez and Gouin-Bonenfant (2024) show that secular declines in required returns can raise Pareto inequality at the top ([*Econometrica*](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA19092)). Fagereng, Gomez, Gouin-Bonenfant, Holm, Moll, and Natvik (2025) emphasize that asset-price *welfare* gains depend on net sales, not holdings—redistribution from young/poor buyers to old/wealthy sellers ([*JPE*](https://doi.org/10.1086/736769)).

**Canada — SFS, capitalization, PBO, StatCan DHEA.**  
Hempel (2025) applies income capitalization to Canada, 1990–2022: top 1% wealth share rises from 16.2% (1990) to 21.3% (2008), then stabilizes near 19.3% (2022)—below U.S.\ levels and below many Pareto-interpolation estimates ([author page](https://hempeleconomics.com/publication/hempel-wealth-inequality-2025/); [PDF](https://hempeleconomics.com/publication/hempel-wealth-inequality-2025/hempel-wealth-inequality-2025.pdf); SSRN earlier version: [doi:10.2139/ssrn.5151980](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.5151980)). The Parliamentary Budget Officer’s High-net-worth Families Database (HFD) combines SFS, rich lists, and Pareto interpolation; Dong (2025) reports a 2023 top 1% share of 23.8% versus 11.0% in the unadjusted SFS ([PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2526-009-S--estimating-top-tail-family-wealth-distribution-in-canada-2025-update--estimation-extremite-superieure-distribution-patrimoine-familial-canada-mises-jour-2025); [PDF](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2025/dpb-pbo/YN5-238-2025-eng.pdf)). Statistics Canada’s Distributions of Household Economic Accounts (DHEA) align SFS microdata with National Balance Sheet Accounts for quarterly wealth distributions ([methodology 13-604-M](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/13-604-m/13-604-m2025001-eng.htm); [SFS overview](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/en/survey/household/2620); [Daily, SFS 2023](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/241029/dq241029a-eng.htm)).

**Implication for this paper.** Quintile- and tenure-based cuts on the SFS PUMF are the right objects; ultra-top cells (P99+) are not. Hempel and PBO bound the top-tail measurement gap without requiring us to reweight the PUMF.

## (b) Wolff’s NIG / balance-sheet inflation-tax metrics

Classical and modern work shows that inflation is a **redistributive balance-sheet event**, not only an income-tax analogue.

**Foundations.**  
Doepke and Schneider (2006) quantify nominal positions and show that moderate surprise inflation transfers wealth from old/rich bondholders to young middle-class mortgagors ([*JPE*](https://doi.org/10.1086/508379); [author PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/mdo738/research/Doepke_Schneider_JPE_06.pdf)). Auclert (2019) embeds Fisher (unexpected inflation), interest-rate exposure, and earnings-heterogeneity channels in monetary transmission when MPCs covary with exposures ([*AER*](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160137); [NBER WP 23451](https://doi.org/10.3386/w23451)).

**Wolff’s accounting (central to this project).**  
Wolff (2021, NBER WP 29392) attributes SCF wealth changes to interest-rate-driven asset revaluation and inflation-driven debt/liquid-asset revaluation, finding that these direct Fed-related balance-sheet effects *reduced* U.S.\ wealth inequality over 1983–2019 despite rising overall Gini ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29392); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3386/w29392); journal version: [*Journal of Economic Issues*](https://doi.org/10.1080/00213624.2024.2308465)). Wolff (2023, NBER WP 31775) defines the inflation tax on income \(IT = INC \cdot INF\), inflation gain on wealth \(IG\), and net inflation gain \(NIG = IG - IT\); empirically, middle wealth quintiles and the ultra-wealthy record large positive \(NIG\) relative to income while the bottom is hit ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31775); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3386/w31775); [PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31775/w31775.pdf)).

**Why \(NIG\) is useful.** It forces income-side “inflation tax” rhetoric to confront leverage, liquid-asset losses, and duration/discount-rate revaluation of stocks, businesses, and bonds—exactly the channels needed to interpret Canadian SFS episodes with falling *and* rising real yields (e.g.\ 2016–2019). It is complementary to Doepke–Schneider net nominal positions (Fisher channel) and Auclert’s redistribution channel (MPC-weighted), but designed for transparent survey accounting by wealth class.

## (c) Canada: affordability, rates, inflation, and other factors

Canadian public debate and central-bank research treat **housing affordability and household cash-flow stress** as first-order, so the *relative* roles of inflation, interest rates, tenure, and geography matter more than a single national CPI narrative.

**Wealth measurement context.** SFS + DHEA + Hempel + PBO HFD (section a) show concentration that is high but milder than U.S.\ top shares, with middle-class housing wealth compressing Gini-type measures even as the extreme top remains hard to measure on the PUMF.

**BoC and mortgage institutions.** Canadian mortgages reset on short fixed terms with long amortizations—unlike U.S.\ 30-year FRMs in Wolff’s housing channel. Bouras, Saldain, Guo, Pugh, and teNyenhuis (2024) estimate that the 2022– rate-hike cycle cut mortgage borrowers’ consumption by about 2.8% by April 2024 (projected ~3.8% by early 2028), with effects lasting longer than the hike cycle itself because higher rates slow principal paydown ([BoC SAN 2024-14](https://doi.org/10.34989/san-2024-14); [PDF](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/san2024-14.pdf)). Related BoC work traces mortgage-rate shocks into house prices and CPI rent, with heterogeneous city-level responses ([e.g.\ SAP 2026-2 PDF](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/sap2026-2.pdf)). Staff work on responding to shelter inflation stresses large *redistributive* effects across renters and owners even when aggregate macro effects are modest ([SAP 2026-5](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/03/staff-analytical-paper-2026-5/)).

**Implication for this paper.** \(NIG\) by wealth quintile, tenure (mortgagor / free-and-clear / renter), age, and analytic region speaks directly to an affordability-focused policy audience: balance-sheet gains from inflation need not imply cash-flow relief when rates rise and Canadian mortgages renew; SHS consumption shares document stock–flow divergence.

## Paste-ready prose (2–4 paragraphs for the paper’s literature section)

> A large literature measures how wealth is distributed and how asset prices and interest rates reshape that distribution. Using historical Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) microdata, Kuhn, Schularick, and Steins (2020) show that middle-class portfolios are housing-intensive while rich households hold business equity, so differential asset-price paths drive U.S.\ wealth inequality even when income inequality moves differently. Federal Reserve work on the SCF and the Distributional Financial Accounts provides the modern infrastructure for these facts: Bricker, Henriques, Krimmel, and Sabelhaus (2016) reconcile survey and administrative top shares, and Batty, Bricker, Briggs, and coauthors construct quarterly DFA wealth distributions consistent with the Financial Accounts (Batty et al.\ 2019, 2022). Related asset-pricing research links equity returns and low required returns to movements in top wealth shares and to welfare redistribution that depends on net sales rather than holdings alone (Gomez 2025; Gomez and Gouin-Bonenfant 2024; Fagereng et al.\ 2025). For Canada, Hempel (2025) capitalizes incomes to estimate a top 1% wealth share that peaked near 21% before the Global Financial Crisis and stood near 19% in 2022—below U.S.\ levels—while the Parliamentary Budget Officer’s High-net-worth Families Database implies a still higher corrected top share (Dong 2025), and Statistics Canada’s Distributions of Household Economic Accounts align the Survey of Financial Security with national balance-sheet totals.
>
> Against this measurement backdrop, a complementary literature treats inflation as a redistributive balance-sheet event. Doepke and Schneider (2006) document large net nominal positions and show that surprise inflation transfers wealth from old bondholders toward young middle-class debtors. Auclert (2019) embeds Fisher, interest-rate exposure, and earnings-heterogeneity channels in the transmission of monetary policy when marginal propensities to consume covary with exposures. Wolff (2021, 2023) operationalizes related ideas in transparent SCF accounting identities. Defining an inflation tax on income and an inflation gain on assets and debts, he constructs the net inflation gain \(NIG = IG - IT\) and finds that U.S.\ middle wealth quintiles—and, under his assumptions, the ultra-wealthy—often record large positive \(NIG\) relative to income, while bottom quintiles are hurt; related interest-rate and inflation revaluations also reduced measured wealth inequality over 1983–2019 even as overall inequality rose for other reasons. These \(NIG\) metrics are useful precisely because they force income-side “inflation tax” rhetoric to confront leverage, liquid-asset losses, and duration-sensitive revaluation of equities, businesses, and bonds.
>
> Canada’s policy debate places special weight on housing affordability and household cash-flow stress, so the relative roles of inflation, interest rates, tenure, and geography are first-order. Canadian mortgages typically renew on short fixed terms, so rate cycles transmit to payments more quickly than under U.S.\ 30-year fixed-rate contracts; Bank of Canada staff work finds material and persistent consumption effects of the recent hike cycle on mortgagors (Bouras et al.\ 2024). This paper adapts Wolff’s \(NIG\) framework to the Canadian Survey of Financial Security. Because the SFS lacks an SCF-style high-income oversample, we emphasize wealth quintiles and tenure rather than ultra-top cells, using Hempel (2025) and PBO estimates only to bound top-tail understatement. The Canadian application is informative both where it echoes Wolff—positive middle-quintile \(NIG\)/income in many episodes—and where institutions differ, including free-and-clear versus mortgagor patterns and periods of rising real yields.
