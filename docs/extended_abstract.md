# Extended abstract

*Editable extended abstract (~650–800 words). Short counterpart: [`abstract.md`](abstract.md).*

**Motivation.** Public debate on inflation often treats rising prices as a pure tax on real incomes. Edward N. Wolff’s NBER Working Papers 31775 and 29392 show that this view is incomplete: inflation also devalues nominal debt and, when it lowers real discount rates relative to nominal rates, can raise the present value of equities, businesses, and bonds. Wolff defines the inflation gain on wealth (\(IG\)), the inflation tax on income (\(IT\)), and the net inflation gain \(NIG = IG - IT\). In U.S. Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) evidence, middle wealth quintiles record large positive \(NIG\)—on the order of two-thirds of income over a long historical span—while bottom quintiles suffer large negative \(NIG\), and ultra-wealthy households can lose on net despite asset gains because income taxes are large and leverage is low. Inflation also reduced measured wealth inequality and the racial wealth gap in the SCF setting. This paper asks whether Canadian Survey of Financial Security (SFS) accounting yields the same qualitative story, and where Canadian data force different conclusions.

**Method.** We adapt Wolff’s marketable net-worth concept to SFS economic-family public-use microdata for 1999–2023 (six waves), excluding vehicles and defined-benefit employer pensions. Non-registered mutual funds are allocated 60/40 equity/bonds. Registered RRSP/RRIF/TFSA totals lack asset-class detail, so we follow the Parliamentary Budget Officer’s TFSA methodology and copy each household’s non-registered financial mix into registered accounts (60/30/10 fallback if none). Macro inputs are Statistics Canada CPI, Government of Canada 10-year yields, and five-year mortgage rates. Annual \(NIG\) is reported by wealth quintile; between waves, start-of-period portfolios are held fixed for revaluation. Mortgage-affordability schedules retain Wolff’s 30-year FRM curve beside Canadian 25-/30-year amortization schedules; renewal risk and pass-through are deferred. Results are stratified by tenure, age, and analytic region (West Coast, Central, Oil Prairies, East Coast).

**Measurement limits.** Relative to the SCF, the SFS has sparse waves, no high-income list sample, and public-use top-coding, so this paper cannot speak reliably about the ultra-wealthy. Hempel (2025) and Parliamentary Budget Officer High-net-worth Families Database corrections raise Canadian top-1% shares above raw SFS figures, yet place Canadian top concentration well below U.S. levels and flatter since about 2008. We emphasize wealth quintiles (and P80–90 where useful) rather than P99; large top-quintile \(NIG\)/income ratios remain fragile. Incomplete race/ethnicity fields mean geography and age replace Wolff’s racial-gap stratification. Registered-mix imputation likely understates equity inside tax-advantaged accounts.

**Main findings.** Middle Canadian wealth quintiles typically show positive \(NIG\) relative to income, while the bottom quintile is near zero or occasionally negative when the income tax dominates thin balance sheets. Large top-quintile \(NIG\) gains and losses are primarily long-duration equity and business revaluation—not leverage: Q5 has the lowest debt/asset ratios, debt devaluation is a small share of \(IG\) at the top, and income tax barely offsets asset-side gains, whereas middle and lower quintiles rely more on debt devaluation. Period results amplify the rate channel: falling real yields (e.g. 2005–2012) raise asset-side gains; 2016–2019, when real yields rose, flips the equity/business channel and turns \(NIG\) negative for several classes (especially Q5) despite positive debt devaluation. A life-cycle gradient is strong—older bands record higher \(NIG\)/income; young renters sit near zero. Geographically, the East Coast is a low outlier relative to Central Canada and British Columbia; over 2019–2023, Oil Prairie households lead on period \(NIG\)/income. Tenure produces a Canadian surprise: free-and-clear owners record *higher* \(NIG\)/income than mortgagors—an asset-stock pattern rather than a pure leverage story.

**Comparison with Wolff’s U.S. results.** Qualitatively, Canada shares Wolff’s headline that inflation is “not a tax” for the middle of the wealth distribution. Wolff’s U.S. bottom quintiles experience large negative \(NIG\); Canadian bottom cells are more often near zero than deeply negative, though still least favoured. Wolff’s ultra-wealthy can lose on net despite asset revaluation; we cannot replicate that claim with the SFS and instead bound top-tail inference with Hempel and PBO estimates. Canadian top concentration is lower and flatter than the U.S. since roughly 2008, so top equity- or business-revaluation operates against a more muted inequality backdrop than in Wolff’s SCF setting. Wolff’s middle-class boom is often told through high leverage, debt devaluation, and housing; the Canadian free-and-clear advantage shows that middle-class gains need not be leverage-driven. Where Wolff reports inflation compressing the U.S. racial wealth gap, we substitute age and regional gradients—East Coast weakness versus Central/BC, and Oil Prairie leadership in the recent high-inflation interval. Institutional adaptations (PBO registered mix, 60/40 mutual funds, Canadian mortgage affordability beside the Wolff FRM benchmark, deferred renewal risk) mark this as a Canadian adaptation, not a one-to-one SCF replication.

**Consumption appendix.** Core \(NIG\) remains SFS-only. The Survey of Household Spending for 2017, 2019, and 2023 provides a recent cash-flow lens: necessities and shelter shares by income, tenure, age, and region, using income×tenure proxies (no wealth quintiles on the SHS). Stock and flow diverge: free-and-clear owners combine high SFS \(NIG\)/income with low SHS necessities shares; renters show the opposite. Household (SHS) versus economic-family (SFS) units, and the post-2010 SHS redesign, preclude splicing to the full 1999–2023 balance-sheet history.

**Contribution.** The paper delivers a transparent Canadian adaptation of Wolff’s \(NIG = IG - IT\) methodology, documents middle-quintile gains qualitatively similar to U.S. findings while stressing top-tail limits, and highlights tenure, age, regional, and stock–flow consumption patterns that refine how the inflation-tax metaphor travels across the border.
