\begin{abstract}
This paper adapts Edward Wolff's accounting framework for the ``inflation tax'' and the balance-sheet effects of interest rates to Canadian household microdata. Using the Survey of Financial Security (1999--2023), we construct Wolff-style marketable net worth, consolidate registered and mutual-fund holdings into equity and fixed-income buckets, and compute the inflation gain on wealth ($IG$), the inflation tax on income ($IT$), and the net inflation gain $NIG=IG-IT$ by wealth quintile. Preliminary results show that middle wealth quintiles typically record positive net inflation gains relative to income---driven by debt devaluation and, when real yields fall, asset revaluation---while the bottom quintile is more exposed to the income tax relative to thin balance sheets. We also report period revaluations and Gini counters in the spirit of Wolff's interest-rate paper. Canadian barriers include sparse survey waves, limited top-tail coverage, incomplete race/ethnicity fields, and mortgage-market institutions that differ from the U.S.\ 30-year fixed-rate market.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Public debate on inflation emphasizes real-income erosion. Wolff (2023) argues that this ``inflation tax'' on income must be paired with balance-sheet gains: inflation reduces the real burden of nominal debt and can raise the present value of claims discounted at real interest rates. The net inflation gain ($NIG$) can therefore be positive for leveraged middle-class households even when real incomes are squeezed.

This draft implements that accounting for Canada. The empirical engine lives in the accompanying repository and reads the Canadian Survey of Financial Security (SFS) rather than the U.S.\ Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF).

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Definitions (Wolff 2023)}

Let $INF$ denote CPI inflation, $INC$ household income, $LIQ$ liquid assets, and $DBT$ debt. Stocks, business equity, and bonds are revalued using a Fisher wedge between nominal and real discount rates (with floors to avoid explosive $1/r$ terms in the low-rate Canadian sample). Then
\begin{align}
IG &= \Delta STK + \Delta BUS + \Delta BND - LIQ\cdot INF + DBT\cdot INF,\\
IT &= INC\cdot INF,\\
NIG &= IG - IT.
\end{align}
Wealth is marketable net worth excluding vehicles and defined-benefit pension wealth. Because the SFS reports registered-account \emph{totals} without asset-class detail, we follow the Parliamentary Budget Officer's TFSA methodology and set each household's RRSP/RRIF/TFSA look-through mix equal to its non-registered financial mix in the SFS.\footnote{Office of the Parliamentary Budget Officer, \emph{The Tax-Free Savings Account} (Ottawa): ``TFSA portfolio composition is assumed to be identical to those observed in non-registered investment accounts of the Survey of Financial Security.'' We apply the same rule to RRSP, RRIF, and other retirement balances. Tax-efficient asset location implies this likely \emph{understates} the equity share inside registered plans---especially TFSAs---so stock-channel inflation gains are conservative on that margin.}

\subsection{Interest rates and inequality (Wolff 2021)}

Between SFS waves we hold start-of-period portfolios fixed and attribute wealth changes to movements in real GoC 10-year yields, bond duration effects, cumulative inflation on liquid assets and debt, and (separately) a Canadian mortgage-affordability house-price factor based on five-year terms and 25-year amortizations.

\subsection{Data}

SFS economic-family PUMF waves: 1999, 2005, 2012, 2016, 2019, 2023. Macro inputs: Statistics Canada CPI; Bank of Canada 10-year yields; conventional five-year mortgage rates. Survey weights ($pweight$) are used throughout.

\section{Barriers and limitations}

Key barriers relative to Wolff's SCF setting include: (i) only six survey years; (ii) no SCF-style high-income oversample; (iii) incomplete race/ethnicity measures on the PUMF; (iv) need to impute equity/bond splits inside registered accounts; (v) Canadian fixed-term mortgages rather than U.S.\ 30-year FRMs. The Survey of Household Spending is reserved for optional consumption-incidence extensions and is not required for core $NIG$.
